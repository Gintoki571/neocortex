# Testing effect, power-law forgetting and spacing (Roediger & Karpicke 2006; Wixted & Carpenter 2007; Carpenter et al. 2008; Mooney et al. 2018)
Sources read: Roediger & Karpicke, "Test-enhanced learning" (https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2006-roediger.pdf, Exp 1-2, Fig 1); Mooney, Sun, Bomgardner, "Predicting recall probability to adaptively prioritize study" (https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.00111, Eq 1-6). Wixted & Carpenter 2007 and Carpenter 2008 were not fetchable; their formula and claim come from search-result summaries (secondary, marked).

**Problem it solves.** What makes a memory durable: re-reading vs retrieving; and what shape does forgetting have.

**Key results (read).** Roediger & Karpicke 2006, passages recalled after a delay: after 5 min restudy beats testing (81% vs 75%), but after **2 days testing 68% vs restudy 54%** and after **1 week 56% vs 42%** (crossover interaction); in Exp 2 repeated studying (SSSS, 14.2 readings) was worse than one study + repeated recall (STTT, 3.4 readings) at one week, while subjects in the study-heavy condition *predicted* they would remember better. So *successful retrieval* strengthens a trace more than re-exposure, and the benefit appears in the long-term curve.
**Forgetting shape (secondary).** Retention follows a power function `P(recall) = m (1 + h t)^(-f)` (Wickelgren / Wixted & Carpenter 2007), not an exponential; Carpenter et al. 2008 found testing reduced the forgetting rate f more than restudy in 2 of 3 experiments (via search summary of the paper abstract). ACT-R's `B = ln sum t^-d` is the cumulative form of the same power law (`actr-base-level.md`). Mooney et al. 2018 (Quizlet) fit a logistic model `logit pi(t) = beta + sum w_i c_i + sum w_j ln(t_now - t_j) + sum w_k ln(t_{k+1} - t_{k+2}) + ...` (Eq 6): recall probability linear in the *log* of time since each previous trial (power-law-like), plus a spacing term in the log of the gap between the two most recent trials; a "recurrent power law" model beat it (Fig 2, AUC).

**What an SQLite + embedding system implements (LLM-free).**
1. **Retrieval-induced strengthening** (not mere insertion): log an access event only when a memory is *used*, with graded weights: surfaced in top-k = 0.3, agent follow-up (`follow`, cite, or `remember` referencing its id) = 1.0, agent-marked "useful/wrong" via an optional feedback argument = +1 / -1. Implement as `accesses(memory_id, t, weight)` and compute `B = ln(sum w_k (now - t_k)^-0.5)` (ACT-R) as a rank feature. nc-e45 already covers "access reinforcement/decay"; this card supplies the parameterisation (power law, d = 0.5, weights) and the evidence that retrieval beats re-exposure, so the *retrieval-success* signal must be separated from *index insertion* and from *re-ingest of the same text* (a duplicate `remember` is restudy, not testing: do not boost as much).
2. **Spacing**: increase strength more when the gap since the previous access is long (ACT-R's spacing-effect extension makes decay d_k depend on activation at the time of the k-th access; (inference, not read)): `w_k = 1 + c * ln(1 + gap_k/day)`. Cheap, monotone.
3. **Forgetting without deleting**: use the power-law activation only to rank and to schedule archive (drop vector, keep relation), never to remove current facts, since superseded facts are retained by design.

**Weak / wrong.** The testing effect is for humans learning text; an index has no learning to protect, so the real benefit is "don't let frequently useful memories sink under newer noise" and nothing else. The 5-minute reversal shows recency can beat retrieval at short delays: keep a recency floor. Fits are lab-scale (days-week), extrapolation to months is an assumption.

**What neocortex could steal, concretely.** An `accesses(memory_id,t,weight)` log separating surfaced / used / re-ingested events, ACT-R power-law rank term, spacing bonus (`recall.rs:1021`, `remember.rs::touch_memory`); coordinate with nc-e45. No LLM.
